Here's something most homeowners don't think about until it's too late.

You're handing keys to your home to people you've met maybe once. They're working when you're not there. And they have access to every room. Every drawer. Every closet.

That's not a criticism. That's reality.

Professional cleaning requires access. But that access needs to be managed carefully. This guide covers how to keep your home secure while still getting it cleaned properly.

## The Real Security Risks (Not What Hollywood Shows)

Forget cat burglars. Forget elaborate heists.

The real risks with cleaning access are boring. And that's what makes them dangerous.

**The Snoop**

Someone glances through papers on a desk. Not stealing anything. Just looking. They see a bank statement. A prescription bottle. A private letter. Information walks out in someone's head.

**The Careless One**

Leaves a door unlocked while taking out trash. A back door. Unlocked for 10 minutes. Anyone could have walked in. No one knows.

**The Overly Helpful Person**

Sees a package at the front door. Brings it inside. Leaves it on the counter. But now you think it was stolen. You file a claim. Package shows up later. Embarrassing for everyone.

**The Whoops Moment**

Accidentally throws away something important. A check. A receipt. A key. Not malicious. Just careless. But now you're searching the trash at 10 PM.

> **The common thread?** Most security incidents with cleaners aren't theft. They're accidents, carelessness, or poor training. And they're almost always preventable.

## Before You Hand Over Those Keys (The Setup)

### Step 1: Know Who's Actually Coming

You should have, at minimum:
- Full names of every cleaner assigned to your home
- Background check verification (ask your service)
- Photos of regular staff (supervisor should have these)
- A point of contact for schedule changes

**Red flags:**
- "We'll send whoever's available"
- No background checks
- High turnover (new person every week)
- Can't provide names in advance

### Step 2: Define What They Can and Can't Access

This is critical. And most homeowners skip it.

**Open Access Areas (Clean freely)**
- Living rooms and common areas
- Hallways
- Bathrooms
- Kitchen

**Restricted Access (Clean only with you present)**
- Home office (sensitive documents)
- Master bedroom (jewelry, personal items)
- Safe or security panel area
- Kids' rooms (if you prefer privacy)

**No Access (Locked, not cleaned)**
- Gun safe or valuables closet
- Home safe
- Wine cellar or collection room
- Any room you specifically ask to skip

> **Put this in writing.** Give cleaners a walk-through. Point out restricted areas. Lock what should be locked. Don't assume everyone knows.

### Step 3: Decide How They Get In

**Option A: Your Keys**

You provide a physical key. Track who has it. Collect it if you stop service.

- *Pros:* You control the key. Easy to revoke access.
- *Cons:* Keys get lost. Copies get made.

**Option B: Cleaner's Keys**

Your service issues keys to their staff. You don't track individual keys.

- *Pros:* Less admin for you. Service handles lost keys.
- *Cons:* You don't know who has keys to your home.

**Option C: Smart Lock Access**

Temporary codes that work only during cleaning hours.

- *Pros:* Most secure. Codes can be changed anytime. No physical keys to lose. You get notified when they enter and leave.
- *Cons:* Requires smart lock (not everyone has one).

**Option D: You're Home**

You're present during cleaning. Let them in. Lock up when they leave.

- *Pros:* Maximum control. No keys given out.
- *Cons:* You have to be home. Kind of defeats the purpose.

> **Best practice:** Smart lock with temporary codes if you have one. Cleaner-provided keys if you don't. Never hide a key under the mat (cleaners will find it, and so will everyone else).

## The Security Methods (How to Handle Access Smartly)

### Method 1: Time-Bound Access

Cleaners can only enter during scheduled hours. Before or after? No access.

**How it works:**
- Smart lock codes active 9 AM to 12 PM only
- Or code expires immediately after cleaning is marked complete
- Entry outside hours triggers alert to your phone

### Method 2: Room-by-Room Restrictions

Sensitive rooms stay locked. Cleaners don't get access. Ever.

**Rooms to consider locking:**
- Home office with financial documents
- Master bedroom jewelry drawer
- Safe room or gun safe
- Guest room that doesn't need cleaning

### Method 3: Digital Logs

Smart locks record who entered and when.

**What's tracked:**
- Cleaner name (if using individual codes)
- Time in
- Time out
- Door left open duration (some systems track this)

> **Ask your cleaning service about their access tracking.** If they can't tell you who was in your home and when, that's a problem.

### Method 4: Periodic Code Rotation

Codes change every few months. Even if someone remembered the code, it stops working.

**Implementation:**
- New code for each cleaning (app-based locks)
- Code changes monthly for regular service
- Unique code per cleaner (so you know who came)

## What Should Be Locked (The Obvious and Not-So-Obvious)

**Obvious (Lock These):**
- Jewelry boxes and safes
- Prescription medications
- Firearms
- Spare credit cards or cash
- Passports and birth certificates

**Not-So-Obvious (Also Lock These):**
- Mail (bills, bank statements, personal letters)
- Checkbook
- Spare car keys
- Garage door opener
- Tablet or laptop left on a desk
- Alcohol (if you have a collection or expensive bottles)
- Prescription sunglasses or designer bags

> **Rule of thumb:** If you'd be upset if it disappeared or someone saw it, lock it up. Even if you trust your cleaners. Especially if you trust your cleaners.

## When You're Not Home (The Vulnerable Hours)

This is when most cleaning happens. And it's when your home is most vulnerable.

**The Good Practice Checklist:**

- [ ] Alarms are disarmed for cleaning window, re-armed after
- [ ] Cleaners have their own alarm code (trackable)
- [ ] Motion sensors in off-limits rooms are active
- [ ] Security cameras cover entry points (visible, not hidden)
- [ ] Cleaning supervisor does a security check before leaving (doors locked, windows closed)

**The "Oh No" Checklist (What to do after an incident):**

- [ ] Change all access codes immediately
- [ ] Review smart lock entry logs
- [ ] Check security camera footage
- [ ] Notify your cleaning service
- [ ] Decide if you want to continue with them (most issues are accidents, not crimes)

## Red Flags (When to Worry)

**Watch for these patterns:**

| What You See | What It Might Mean | What To Do |
|--------------|--------------------|----------------|
| Same cleaner always works late alone | Could be fine. Could be snooping. | Ask for a team or switch days randomly |
| Things feel "moved" in closed rooms | Someone went where they shouldn't | Lock that room next time. See if it happens again. |
| Door found unlocked when you return | Carelessness or rushed exit | Require text confirmation that door is locked |
| Cleaners in areas you said to skip | Curiosity or worse | Remind of restricted areas. One warning only. |
| High turnover on your account | Service can't keep staff | Ask for permanent team. Switch services if it continues. |
| Can't reach anyone after hours | No emergency contact | Require 24/7 phone number. Test it. |

## The Human Factor (Because People Are People)

Here's the thing about home security.

Most issues aren't theft. They're someone being nice. Or careless. Or just not thinking.

**Example 1: The Helpful Cleaner**

Sees a package on your porch. "Oh, I'll bring this inside." Brings it in. Leaves it on the kitchen table. But you were expecting that package. Now you think it was stolen. You file a claim. Package shows up later. Awkward.

**Fix:** "Leave all packages outside" in your cleaning instructions. Put it in writing.

**Example 2: The Chatty Cleaner**

Talks to your neighbor. "Oh, the Smiths are on vacation next week, that's why we're doing a deep clean." Now your neighbor knows you're away. Maybe they're trustworthy. Maybe they're not.

**Fix:** Train cleaners not to discuss your schedule with anyone. "I'm not sure" is always the right answer.

**Example 3: The Key Hider**

Cleaner can't find the hidden key. Leaves it under a different flowerpot. Forgets to tell you. Now your key is somewhere you don't know. Anyone could find it.

**Fix:** No hidden keys. Ever. Smart lock or give them a key directly.

## What Your Cleaning Agreement Should Include (Security Section)

Don't assume these things. Put them in writing.

**Required clauses:**
- Background checks for all cleaning staff (and proof available on request)
- No subcontracting without your approval
- Named, consistent team (not rotating randoms)
- Security incident reporting within 24 hours
- Bonding or insurance for theft coverage

**Nice-to-have clauses:**
- Periodic re-screening of existing staff
- Non-disclosure agreement for anything they see or hear
- Two-person team for larger homes (accountability)

> **Ask your cleaning service for their security policy.** If they don't have one written down, find another service.

## What About Cameras?

This is a sensitive one. Some homeowners love cameras. Cleaners hate them. Where's the middle ground?

**The Cleaner's Perspective:**
They don't want to be watched while working. It feels creepy. Like they're not trusted.

**Your Perspective:**
It's your home. You have a right to know what happens when you're not there.

**The Compromise:**
- Tell cleaners you have cameras (surprise cameras are illegal in some states)
- Put cameras in common areas only (not bathrooms or bedrooms)
- Don't watch in real time (review if something seems off)
- Most good cleaners won't mind visible cameras. Hidden ones? Different story.

> **Bottom line:** Cameras are fine. Secret cameras are not. Be upfront.

## The Bottom Line (In Plain English)

You want a clean home. Cleaners need access to do that.

But access comes with risk. Not huge risk. Not "install a panic room" risk. But real risk that needs to be managed.

**The three things that matter most:**

1. **Know who's in your home** – Names, faces, background checks. No surprises.

2. **Lock what you care about** – If it matters, lock it. Even if you trust them.

3. **Have a plan for problems** – Change codes. Review logs. Talk to your service. Don't hope it goes away.

Home cleaning security isn't complicated. But it does require thinking about things most people don't want to think about.

Like whether the nice person scrubbing your toilet is also reading the mail on your counter.

Probably not. But "probably" isn't a security strategy.
